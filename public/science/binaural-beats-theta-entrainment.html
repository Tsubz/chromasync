Binaural Beats: How They Work and What the Evidence Says

A beat created in the brainstem, not in the air
Key point: when each ear hears a tone of slightly different frequency, the brain perceives a slow beat at the difference between them. This works only with headphones. Whether these beats actually entrain brainwaves is uncertain: a 2023 systematic review found supporting results in 5 of 14 studies. A 2019 meta-analysis reported modest effects on anxiety and some cognitive tasks.

1. A beat made by the brain

Two tones of close frequencies played through one speaker interfere in the air and create an ordinary (acoustic) beat. A binaural beat is different: each tone reaches only one ear, and the beat arises in the brainstem, where neurons of the superior olivary complex compare the timing of the signals from both ears (Oster, 1973). 

Left ear:  105.25 Hz ──►┐
                        ├──► Superior olivary complex ──► perceived beat: 5.5 Hz
Right ear: 110.75 Hz ──►┘

The effect requires headphones and works best with low carrier tones (below about 1000 Hz). If both tones leak into the same ear, the result is partly an ordinary beat instead.

2. What the evidence shows

The idea is that a 5.5 Hz beat nudges the brain toward theta rhythms and a 10 Hz beat toward alpha rhythms. Research does not settle this. Of 14 EEG studies in a 2023 systematic review, 5 supported entrainment, 8 contradicted it and 1 was mixed, with very different methods across studies (Ingendoh et al., 2023). A 2019 meta-analysis found small-to-moderate benefits on anxiety, memory and attention tasks and on pain perception, independent of whether brainwaves were entrained (Garcia-Argibay et al., 2019).

3. What Chromasync does

Chromasync plays each voice of its ambient chord as a left/right pair separated by the selected beat (4, 5.5 or 10 Hz). Each tone is sent to one ear only, and both ears share the same slow pitch drift, so the difference stays exactly at the chosen value.
