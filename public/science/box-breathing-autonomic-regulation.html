Box Breathing (4-4-4-4): What It Does and What It Does Not

Four equal 4-second phases: in, hold, out, hold
Key point: box breathing alternates four equal 4-second phases. It slows breathing to under 4 breaths per minute and avoids the over-breathing that comes with stress. In a randomized trial, daily box breathing improved mood, though less than cyclic sighing. A 2025 comparison found it raised heart rate variability less than breathing at 6 breaths per minute.

1. The four phases

Box breathing, also called square breathing, is close to the yogic sama vritti ("equal breath") and is widely taught to people who must stay calm under pressure. One cycle lasts 16 seconds, which is 3.75 breaths per minute.

2. Why the pauses help

Under stress, people tend to breathe fast and shallow. This blows off carbon dioxide (hypocapnia), which narrows the blood vessels of the brain and can cause dizziness, tingling and a sense of panic. A slow rhythm with pauses keeps the volume of air breathed per minute low, so carbon dioxide stays in its normal range (Homma & Masaoka, 2008).

3. What the evidence shows

In a randomized trial with 108 participants practising 5 minutes a day for a month, box breathing, cyclic sighing and cyclic hyperventilation all improved mood. Cyclic sighing produced the largest improvement in positive mood and the largest drop in resting breathing rate (Balban et al., 2023). In a 2025 laboratory comparison, box breathing raised heart rate variability less than breathing at 6 breaths per minute, and no single session changed blood pressure or mood (Marchant et al., 2025).

Box breathing remains a simple, structured way to slow down. If the holds feel uncomfortable, shorten them.
