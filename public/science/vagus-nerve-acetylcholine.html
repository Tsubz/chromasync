The Vagus Nerve and the Long Exhale

The respiratory gate, acetylcholine, and long-exhale breathing
Key point: the vagus nerve slows the heart mostly during exhalation, which is why heart rate naturally falls on every out-breath. Patterns with a long exhale, such as 4-7-8, lean on this mechanism. They are widely used to wind down, but direct clinical evidence for 4-7-8 is still limited to small studies.

1. The respiratory gate

Signals from the vagus nerve to the heart are not constant. Breathing circuits in the brainstem partly block them during inhalation and let them through during exhalation (Eckberg, 2003). This is why the heart speeds up slightly on each in-breath and slows on each out-breath, a normal pattern called respiratory sinus arrhythmia.

2. How the vagus nerve slows the heart

At the heart's natural pacemaker (the sinoatrial node), vagal nerve endings release acetylcholine. It binds to muscarinic M2 receptors, which open potassium channels (IK,ACh) and lower cyclic AMP inside pacemaker cells. Each beat then takes slightly longer to trigger, and heart rate falls within a beat or two.

In the 4-7-8 pattern popularised by Dr. Andrew Weil, the exhale lasts twice as long as the inhale. Gerritsen and Band (2018) propose that lengthening the out-breath is one way contemplative practices increase vagal activity. In a small trial, 4-7-8 breathing increased high-frequency HRV in rested young adults (Vierra et al., 2022). In a 2025 comparison, it raised overall HRV less than breathing at 6 breaths per minute (Marchant et al., 2025).

The 7-second hold is demanding for beginners. Shorten it if needed, or try Chromasync's gentler Deep Unwind (4-2-6) pattern.
